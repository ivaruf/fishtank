# Fishtank

A tiny 3D aquarium game: swim, eat smaller fish, grow, and try not to become lunch. Babylon.js, vanilla JavaScript, Blender-made fish, and no server at all — solo runs entirely in your browser and multiplayer goes peer to peer. Nothing is bundled, compiled or minified; Babylon.js and PeerJS load from pinned CDN URLs, and `tools/build-static.mjs` assembles the GitHub Pages build.

## Run

It is a static site, so anything that serves files will do. Node 22 is needed only for the tests and the build.

```sh
npm run serve        # python3 -m http.server 3000, from the repo root
```

Then open http://localhost:3000. The root `index.html` hops to `client/`, where the app lives, because its modules import `../../shared/` and so `client/` and `shared/` have to be siblings with neither of them the root — the deployed site takes the same hop, and that file is committed rather than generated so both do it identically. Editing any file and reloading is the whole loop; there is nothing to restart and nothing to rebuild. `npm run build:static` assembles `_site`, which is what GitHub Pages publishes, and is worth running before a push if you have touched the layout it assembles.

One caveat with serving the repository directly: everything in it is served, `.git/` and `node_modules/` included. That is fine on localhost and worth remembering before pointing another device on the wifi at it — for that, serve `_site` instead, which contains only what deploys.

Type a name and pick which of the fourteen fish you want to be; the choice is remembered and the chosen fish swims large beside the menu. Then **Dive in solo** for a private aquarium, or **Play with friends** to host a tank or join one.

There are two ways to play together and both of them are peer to peer: one player's browser runs the simulation and everyone else connects straight to it. Whoever presses **Host a tank** gets a code of three pictures — 🐟 🦆 🐝 ⛵ 🚗 🍕 🌲 ⭐ 🌙 🔑 🎩 ⚽ — shown with its words underneath and a Copy button. The other player taps those three pictures on the dialpad, or types the words. See [the peer-to-peer notes](docs/P2P.md) for how two browsers find each other with nothing of ours in between, and for the honest limits: no TURN, so roughly a tenth of network pairs never connect; no host migration, so the host closing the tab ends the game; and the host can cheat, because the host is authoritative.

Two devices on the same wifi still need the internet to _find_ each other, because the signalling broker is on it. Two tabs in one browser do not — they meet over `BroadcastChannel`, which is how most of this gets tested. A `<meta name="signal-url">` relay is the way to make a genuinely offline LAN work, and nothing here ships one.

## Controls and rules

- W/S move forward/backward along your camera aim. A/D strafe left/right. Release movement controls to stand still. Aim up or down and hold W to change depth.
- Move the mouse over the aquarium to aim. Click the aquarium to capture the pointer for unlimited turning; Esc releases it. Hover aiming remains available if pointer lock is unavailable.
- Touch controls are the keyboard in another shape: the left half is a stick that appears under your thumb and moves you — push it to swim, forward, back or sideways, and let go to stop — while dragging anywhere on the right half aims the camera, exactly as the mouse does. Movement and aim are independent, so you can swim one way and look another, and look up or down and push forward to rise or dive. Standing still is the default. Phones play in landscape only: held upright they show a full-screen "turn your phone" prompt, rendering pauses and your fish holds still until you turn it.
- Tap the ⛶ button in the header to enter fullscreen; tap it again to exit. It keeps the HUD and touch sticks visible and is shown when the browser supports page fullscreen.
- Input clears on focus loss, and whoever is running the tank stops your movement after 350 ms without fresh input. Movement diagonals are normalized; partial stick deflection gives partial speed.
- Swim headfirst into a lighter fish to eat it. The food chain is binary: anything heavier than you can eat you and anything lighter is food, so every fish is either brighter (food) or darker (danger); only an exact tie, such as two fresh spawns, is neutral. Visual radius grows with the cube root of mass, so eight times the mass is twice the size: the number in the HUD climbs far faster than the fish does, and every meal pops the fish briefly bigger so the growth is something you feel rather than something you could only measure.
- Wild fish play by the same rule against you: any heavier one can eat you, and those drift toward lighter players within about nine units, swimming a little faster while they do. Give the big ones room. Other players' names float above their fish, except while they hide in the kelp.
- Being the biggest fish is risky: a faulty water filter stands in the back-left corner with one big red button. It is dead for the first minute of a round, then its lamp turns green, it crackles, and any fish that swims into the button zaps the heaviest player in the tank (the heaviest wild fish when you play alone): stunned for 2.5 seconds, 30% lighter, with the lost mass scattered around it as small snacks of its own species that anyone can eat. The filter then recharges for 15 seconds, lamp red; pressing it early or during the recharge only buzzes.
- Duck into a kelp forest to hide: low inside one of the thirteen clusters you are not hunted and the HUD says so, though a fish that blunders into you still eats you. Cluster positions live in `shared/config.js`, so the tank you see and the tank being simulated agree about where cover is.
- The rocks, the driftwood, the pots, the wreck and the stone arch are solid, and what fits through them depends on how big you are. A fresh fish ducks under the arch and slips into the broken hull of the wreck over its low near side; past about mass 32 the arch closes to you and you have to swim over it, and the wreck's interior shuts too. So the tank opens up small routes that close as you eat — worth remembering when something heavier is behind you. Kelp and plants block nothing.
- Eating is a cartoon chomp: the head rears back with the mouth gaping wide, snaps shut with a squash and a puff of bubbles, and the eaten fish rolls over, tumbles and shrinks into the predator's mouth before it vanishes.
- New and respawning players have three seconds of protection: they cannot eat or be eaten. When you are eaten the camera swings to the predator's mouth to watch it chew you down before "You became lunch" appears; you return after five seconds. Score survives death.
- Rounds (two minutes solo, one to five in a lobby) rank players by score, then mass, so no lead lasts long; the current leader wears a small gold crown (and a 👑 on their name label) for everyone to see. Results also show the largest fish and stay up until someone presses **Start the next round**.
- Leave tank returns to the menu. After a connection failure, join again to start as a new fish.
- Esc, or the ✕ button, pauses to a panel you can see the tank through, so the fish keep swimming while you choose. Graphics live there rather than on the HUD, so nothing but the game is on screen while you are playing.
- A room code is three pictures — 🐟 🦆 🐝 ⛵ 🚗 🍕 🌲 ⭐ 🌙 🔑 🎩 ⚽, one thing per category so nobody has to ask which fish it was — so **Play with a friend** can be joined by tapping three big keys on a dialpad rather than typing anything. It is one code, not two systems: the host's lobby shows the pictures with `BOAT FISH STAR` underneath and a Copy button, so whoever is sharing it pastes words into a chat while whoever is joining taps pictures. Typing still works and is deliberately forgiving — any separators, any case, two-letter prefixes, or the emoji pasted straight in. The third tap joins on the spot, because "now press Join" is exactly the extra step this exists to remove. Three pictures is 1,728 codes rather than the 20,736 four would give, which is a deliberate trade: getting in easily matters more than the remote chance of reaching a stranger's tank, and a code already in use is reported rather than silently wrong.

## Architecture

`shared/world.js` owns movement, NPC wandering and hunting, head-biased collisions, hard-scenery collisions, growth, deaths, respawning, and rounds. It simulates at 30 Hz and publishes at 15 Hz. There is exactly one implementation of the game and three places it can run: in your own tab for solo (`client/js/local.js`), in a host's Web Worker for a peer game (`client/js/host-worker.js`), or on the host's main thread as a fallback. `main.js` cannot tell the three apart, because each presents the same interface, so the simulation cannot drift between them. Shared tuning values live in `shared/config.js`.

There used to be a fourth: `server.js`, an authoritative Node/WebSocket server with three persistent tanks, deployed to Render behind a CI gate. It is gone, and with it the tank browser, the game list, `maxPlayers` as a bandwidth argument, and a deploy that ended every live game by restarting the process. What replaced it is the thing that was always underneath — one player's browser is the authority — so the deletion took the transport and left the game. `git log` has it if the reasoning is ever needed; the branch it lived on is `main`.

Snapshots are quantised and packed. `World.snapshot()` rounds positions to 1 cm, angles to about 0.06° and mass to two decimals on the way out only — the simulation keeps full doubles, so rounding can never feed back into movement or into who outweighs whom — and `shared/snapshot-codec.js` packs a frame into binary, 19.8 KB down to 1.6 KB. That codec is not premature: a WebRTC data channel cannot compress (`permessage-deflate` is a WebSocket feature) and has a 16 KB interoperable message limit, so a JSON frame would simply not fit a full tank. `maxPlayers` is sixteen because that is what one hosting device can carry, not because of anything a server was paying for.

The game is installable and plays with no network at all. `client/sw.js` precaches the shell — the markup, every client and shared module, and the manifest — and `client/manifest.webmanifest` makes it a standalone, landscape app with maskable icons. Code and markup are fetched network-first so an online player always gets the deployed build, which is what the `build <sha>` line on the menu reports; models, sounds and the Babylon bundles are served from cache and revalidated in the background, since they are megabytes and change only when they are rebuilt. `version.json` is never cached, because a stale copy of the build report is the one thing that would make the "you are running old code" warning useless. Because solo runs entirely in the browser, an installed tank started from a home screen with the network off boots and plays. Registration is fire-and-forget and skipped outside a secure context, so plain-http LAN play is unaffected.

Babylon loads from jsDelivr, pinned to the exact version in `package.json` and locked with an SRI hash, which takes 7.9 MB off the host and is often already in a visitor's cache; the `/vendor/` copies `build-static.mjs` writes remain wired as a fallback, so a CDN outage, a blocked request, an SRI mismatch or plain LAN play with no internet all fall through to the local bytes without changing script order. A test asserts the SRI hash still matches the local file, so a Babylon upgrade cannot leave the two out of step. Compression and caching of everything else is GitHub Pages' business now rather than ours.

`client/js/world.js` creates the aquarium (white coral sand, noise-displaced rocks, kelp forests whose merged vertices sway every frame, a framed glass tank with a light bar and a rippling surface) and the evening office it stands in, all procedural geometry with small canvas-drawn textures. The tank's light bar is a spot light and the desk and floor lamps are point lights with a glow layer, over a faint blue ambient; room materials ignore the water fog so they read as air beyond the glass. `fish.js` loads every Blender fish once at the current quality tier and hands out GPU-instanced copies with bite, death and threat-tint hooks; `scenery.js` stands the Blender scenery pack on the sand as frozen instances; `sand.js` lays the authored seabed at the top two tiers; `environment.js` adds the authored office and filter detail layers there too and disposes them again below that; `effects.js` owns the shared bubble-puff particles. Meshes still decide nothing: the rocks it draws come from the shared table in `shared/scenery.js`, and the boxes the simulation stops fish against come from the same one, so the two cannot part company. `controls.js` reads keyboard/touch intentions. `codes.js` holds the picture codes. `main.js` interpolates positions and angles, smooths the camera, and updates safe text-only UI. No client prediction or lag compensation is implemented.

Protocol, host to guest and back, described in full in [the peer-to-peer notes](docs/P2P.md): a guest sends `JOIN {name, species}` and `INPUT {forward, strafe, yaw, pitch}` with normalized movement axes and camera angles in radians, plus `NEXT_ROUND` from the results screen and, in a lobby, `READY {ready}`; the host answers `WELCOME {id, protocol, room}`, a roster over the reliable channel, and packed `WORLD_STATE` frames over the unreliable one. Events carry `NPC_EATEN` / `PLAYER_EATEN {predator, prey, label, grew}`, `FILTER_ARMED`, `BUZZ {by}`, `ZAP {target, by, lost}` or `ROUND_END`; frames carry `filter {armed, cooldown}`, a fish's remaining `stunned` seconds, and in a lobby `lobby {host, duration, ready}`. The host ignores every position, size and score a guest claims, clamps its axes, discards non-finite numbers, and replaces an unknown species with a valid one — a guest cannot assert its own state. `PROTOCOL` is compared on join, and since host and guest are two copies of the same code, a mismatch means one of them has not reloaded.

## Tests

```sh
npm test
```

Tests cover the lobby (host, ready gate, host-only length and start, back to the lobby after results, host handover), eating thresholds/head collision, protection, growth, NPC movement, hunting and predation, kelp cover, the filter's arming delay, zap, recharge, stun and food chunks, respawn, PvP death and respawn, all tank boundaries, round transitions, private solo rooms, malformed messages, input authority, and a guest joining, leaving and rejoining a host. Two real peers are driven over an in-process channel; `tests/peer.test.js` covers the host/guest handshake, the roster, the packed frames and the capacity cap without a browser or a network.

`tests/fish.test.js` loads the real model files on Babylon's NullEngine and checks orientation, instancing, material conversion, the swim clip, bite and death animation state, threat tints through the per-instance color buffer, disposal, and the procedural fallback. `tests/codes.test.js` covers the picture codes: that a generated code survives the parser unchanged in all three of its forms, that the forgiving spellings all land in the same room, that ambiguous or half-typed ones are refused rather than guessed at, and that an old six-consonant code still passes through. `tests/scenery.test.js` covers the hard scenery: which fish fit under the arch and at what mass the gate closes, that the wreck admits a small fish and refuses a big one while its planked flank refuses everybody, that boulders are solid at every point including their exact centre, that the filter's button and the plants stay swimmable, that nothing spawns inside anything, and that the rock table the client draws is the one the simulation collides against. `tests/environment.test.js` loads the real office and zapper GLBs and covers quality switching, material cleanup, stale load rejection and desk alignment.

What the tests cannot cover is two separate devices finding each other through the broker, which is the one part with a third party in it. That is a manual check, and [the peer-to-peer notes](docs/P2P.md) say what to look for.

Manual browser check: host in one window and join from another, move and aim independently, eat small NPCs, compare growth across windows, then eat a smaller player. Verify stopping on key release, dual-stick movement and aiming on a phone, respawn, and results after two minutes. Browser/device coverage is not implied by automated tests.

## The brand art

Every shipped brand asset comes out of one painting. `assets/brand/fishtank-title.png` is it exactly as delivered — 2000 × 819, background and all — and `tools/make-brand.py` cuts the rest:

```sh
python3 tools/make-brand.py
```

| what                    | from                            | where                                        |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| the menu's title        | the whole lockup                | `client/assets/brand/fishtank-title.webp`    |
| the in-game corner logo | the wordmark alone              | `client/assets/brand/fishtank-wordmark.webp` |
| the app icons           | the little fish and its bubbles | `client/assets/icons/*.png`                  |

WebP for the two brand images: 147 KB against 564 KB for the same pixels as PNG, which is worth having on the first screen a player sees, and a browser without WebP was never going to manage the WebGL or the data channels either. `docs/title-on-water.png` and `docs/icons.png` are the proof sheets, composited over the colours these are actually seen against, because that is the only background against which a cut-out can be judged.

The keying is a flood fill inward from the border, not a threshold on colour, and that is the whole trick: the letters are outlined in a dark teal close enough to the background that any global threshold eats them, while the background is the only region of that colour touching the border. Filling from the edge takes it and leaves every enclosed dark pixel alone — the outlines, the fish's eye, the counters in the _a_ and the _o_. Alpha then ramps across a band either side of the threshold rather than switching, or the letters pick up the one-pixel dark fringe that gives a badly cut-out logo away.

The corner logo in the header is the painting's own wordmark, cut at the row where the wordmark and the tagline are least tangled. They do interlock — the tagline's ascenders reach up into the wordmark's band and its glow touches them — so neither a straight cut nor connected components separate them. What does is a per-column rule: in the strip where they overlap, keep only the run of ink still connected to the top of the strip, since everything else there belongs to whatever is below it. Without that the corner logo carries a dotted line under the word, which is what the tagline's ascender tips look like at 40 px. It is sized by height at 46 px, which is about the smallest this reads at — the word is legible from around 40 and the tail on the f survives, while 28 is a smudge — stepping down to 34 on a landscape phone. It still hides itself while the menu is up, since the art is already saying the name three times larger immediately below.

The app icons are the little fish and its bubbles rather than any part of the word. A fragment of a letter cannot be an icon here: the letters run into each other, so every square crop of them is visibly clipped, and the whole wordmark in a square is a strip that is unreadable by 32 px. The fish is the one self-contained motif in the piece, it is what the game is about, and it still reads as a fish at 32. Two edges of its crop box are load-bearing and commented as such — the `k`'s bitten edge reaches right into the fish's column but only below it, and the tagline runs on underneath, so a box any taller picks up a speck of it. The maskable icon fills appreciably less of its square than the others, because Android crops it to whatever shape it likes and guarantees only the inner 80%.

## Blender fish models

Fourteen stylized fish live in `client/assets/models/` as glTF Binary: clownfish, blue tang, pufferfish, angelfish, goldfish, betta, shark, butterflyfish, lionfish, wrasse, seahorse, manta ray, royal gramma, and triggerfish, each authored at four quality tiers (`-low.glb`, `.glb`, `-high.glb`, `-hd.glb`). Their Blender sources are in `assets/blender/`, `docs/*.png` are studio renders, and `client/assets/thumbs/*.png` are the small transparent renders the species picker shows. Everything is generated by `tools/blender/create_fish.py` (tested with Blender 5.2):

```sh
# Everything, or just some species, or only the menu thumbnails from saved .blend files
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_fish.py
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_fish.py -- betta shark
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_fish.py -- --thumbnails-only clownfish
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_fish.py -- --hd-only
```

Each file has a root node, a static `BodyMesh`, and a `Tail` pivot whose child fan is animated by a one-second looping wag. Fish face +Z with +Y up around a two-unit body; gameplay radius is applied on the parent transform in the client. Materials are plain PBR colors without textures.

At startup `fish.js` loads all registered species into Babylon asset containers via the locally served glTF loader. Every fish in the tank is then created with `instantiateModelsToScene` using GPU instances, so a hundred NPCs share geometry and materials per species. Since the scene has no environment texture, the exported PBR materials are converted to the same `StandardMaterial` setup as the rocks and plants. Linear glTF colors are gamma-converted, and because the tank's two lights add up to roughly 2.6x on upward faces, each fish material takes 30% of its color from lighting and 40% as same-hue emissive so pale species like the pufferfish keep their color instead of clipping to white. Players choose their species in the menu and send it with `JOIN`; the server validates it, assigns NPC species round-robin, and includes `species` in every snapshot, so all clients agree. Players carry a small glowing crest so they can be told apart from NPCs. The exported swim clip is cloned per fish, started at a random phase, and its speed follows how fast the fish is moving, so idle fish only idle-wag. Each fish has a root node carrying the server transform and a child pose node for client-side bite lunges and death tumbles, so the models need no extra clips. The threat cue writes a per-instance color (`instanceColor`), which the standard shader multiplies into the final color while keeping every fish of a species in one instanced batch.

If a model fails to load the factory falls back to the earlier procedural sphere fish with the same root/swim/dispose contract, and the console names the missing species. To add a species, add a branch to the Blender script, run it for that kind, and append the name to `SPECIES` in `shared/config.js`; the picker, the server, and the loader all read that list. The Blender exporter currently names the clip `Animation` rather than `swim`; the client accepts either and takes the first clip it finds.

The rocks and the hard scenery block fish, sized so a small one fits where a grown one cannot — see the Blender scenery section. The plants, kelp, glass and office remain decoration with no collision at all. NPCs wander and provide food, and the large ones hunt players who stray close. It targets small LAN games, not hardened public matchmaking.

## Blender scenery

Seven static assets dress the sand: `ribbon-grass` (fourteen curved blades on a ring of pebbles), `broadleaf-plant` (ten ridged leaves with pale midribs), `red-stem-plant` (five stems of paired copper and burgundy leaves), `branching-driftwood` (a worn branching trunk with roots, grain and moss), `stone-arch` (an open passage of arch stones on two foundations), `weathered-amphora` (a hollow pot with loop handles, bands and a hairline crack) and `little-shipwreck` (an open broken hull with exposed ribs, surviving deck planks, a snapped mast, a fallen spar and trailing rope). They ship from `client/assets/scenery/` as glTF Binary, their editable sources sit beside the fish in `assets/blender/`, and `docs/scenery-preview.png` is the review sheet. Everything is solid geometry with plain colours — no textures, no images, no alpha cutouts — from 1,360 triangles for the arch to 9,740 for the red-stem plant.

```sh
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_scenery.py
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_shipwreck.py
blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/render_scenery.py
```

`create_scenery.py` builds the first six, `create_shipwreck.py` the wreck (its own script: it is built from bevelled beams rather than plant parts, and it is the only asset with an interior), and `render_scenery.py` renders the sheet from the saved sources rather than rebuilding, so it always shows what was actually exported. `scenery_common.py` holds the paths, the seed and the mesh helpers the three share. Each file is one root empty named after the asset with every mesh parented to it, authored Z-up and exported Y-up with its origin at substrate level, so the client stands it on the sand at y = 0 with no per-asset offset to remember. One fixed seed covers the pack, so pebbles, moss and stone dents land in the same place on every rebuild and the committed `.glb` files do not churn.

`shared/scenery.js` holds the layout, and it is shared rather than client-side precisely because the hard pieces stop fish: the client reads the table to place meshes and the simulation reads it to place walls, so the picture and the physics cannot drift apart. Fourteen placements, each naming an asset, a spot on the sand, a heading and a scale, since the pack is authored small — a plant stands 1.5 to 2.7 units against a two-unit fish — and needs scaling up to read at tank size. Six are landmarks standing inside the rock ring — they were first placed to dodge the nine loose rocks in the middle of the tank and forgot the twenty-two ringing it, which was harmless while this was decoration and became five invisible walls the moment it was not, so each now has at least four units of clear water round its own footprint and the arch has seven, found by searching rather than by eye; the other eight are plants at the foot of eight of the thirteen kelp clusters, read off `PLANTS` so the dressing follows the cover if those positions ever move. Positions keep clear of the filter's corner and of the loose rocks world.js scatters inside the ring, and the layout is a table rather than a random draw so every machine sees the same tank. Each asset loads once and every placement is a GPU instance, world matrices frozen after placement because nothing in the pack moves — the kelp beside it is what sways. Babylon defaults `doNotInstantiate` to true, so the option is passed explicitly; left off, every placement silently clones its geometry instead. Instancing saves geometry rather than batches: an asset is dozens of small meshes, so the pack costs roughly 250 draw calls, and if that ever shows on a slow tablet the lever is merging each asset down to one mesh per material, the way `buildKelp` already merges a whole forest into two. Materials are converted to `StandardMaterial` exactly as the fish are and for the same reason, a little darker (32% of the colour from lighting, 30% as same-hue emissive) so a landmark never out-glows a fish swimming past it.

The hard ones stop fish. Rocks, driftwood, the amphorae, the wreck and the arch are solid; the three plants are dressing and stay swimmable, and cover is still only the kelp clusters, so swimming behind the wreck hides you from nobody. A failed load warns with the asset's name and places the rest — and the collision stands either way, since the server decides it, so a player whose download failed meets the same walls and simply cannot see what they are.

Every solid thing is a box or a boulder and a fish is a sphere of `radius(mass)`, which is the entire physics of this game. A boulder is not a smooth ellipsoid: `rockBump` is the layered-sine displacement the client pushes its sphere's vertices out with, and the resolver evaluates that same function along the ray it is pushing a fish out on, so the dents you can see are the dents you cannot swim into. That matters more than it sounds — the surface ranges from 0.6 to 1.4 of the nominal radius, and an ellipsoid cut to the average let a fresh fish's centre get 1.11 units past the visible skin, which is over half a fish. Nothing in the code says "a small fish may pass": the arch's collision is its real ring of stones, generated from the same formula the Blender script builds it with, and a small fish fits under it while a big one does not because that is what its radius does. It was three boxes at first — two legs and a lintel across the whole span — and that lintel was a lie worth remembering: at the outer edge of an arch the stone is down by the sand, not up at the crown, so the box filled both upper corners of the opening with solid nothing and fish swimming past at crown height hit a wall they could not see. The gate closes at **mass 32.3** — the crown rather than the legs is what shuts, since a fish needs room between the sand and the stone overhead and its centre cannot drop below `r + 0.5`. Fish start at 8, so the arch stops being a shortcut after four or five meals and has to be gone over instead; `tests/scenery.test.js` asserts that number, so moving the arch or changing growth will say so out loud — and it is what pins the arch's scale at 3.8, because the true curved ceiling closed the gate at mass 16 and made the passage something you lose after a single meal. The wreck is a shell rather than a solid for the same reason: its far flank and both ends are closed, the staved-in near side keeps only its bottom strake, so a small fish clears that sill or drops in over the open top and a big one cannot get in at all.

Boxes, not the meshes — nothing that runs a simulation has any business loading a GLB to find out where a plank is. Resolution is depenetration rather than a swept test: at the tank's top speed a fish covers 0.23 units in a tick against volumes metres across, so there is nothing to tunnel through, and pushing out of an overlap is both cheaper and kinder, because only the component pushing into the surface is removed and a fish swimming at a hull slides along it. Wild fish are pushed too, and turn away from whatever they hit — without that a hundred of them spend the round pressed into the wreck and the tank looks broken from outside even though the collision is perfect. A full two-minute round of 100 wild fish costs 0.09 ms a tick and ends with none of them inside anything.

## Audio

Music and effects are Sonic Pi pieces in `tools/audio/`: a calm menu theme ("Below the surface", 60 bpm, 32-beat loop), a gameplay loop ("Feeding time", 100 bpm, 64 beats), and one-shots for your bite, being eaten, a fish eaten nearby (fades with distance), respawning, a predator swimming close (at most every six seconds), the round ending, menu clicks, and the filter's arming chime, too-early buzz and lightning crack. `client/js/audio.js` plays them through Web Audio: loops crossfade between menu and game, effects share a bus, and the 🔊 button in the header mutes everything and remembers it. Audio starts on the first click or key press, as browsers require.

The recorder itself, `render.rb`, is not in this repo: it is generic and shared by every game in the hub, so it lives one level up in `../tools/audio/`. The pieces and `encode.mjs` here are fishtank's own.

Rendering needs Sonic Pi 5 installed (`/Applications/Sonic Pi.app`, override with `SONIC_PI_APP`), ffmpeg on the PATH, and the real audio device, so it cannot run in a sandbox and you will hear it render in real time:

```sh
SP="/Applications/Sonic Pi.app/Contents/Resources/app/server/native/ruby/bin/ruby"
SONIC_PI_PIECES=tools/audio "$SP" ../tools/audio/render.rb /tmp/fishtank-audio music-menu=70 music-game=83 chomp=2 eaten=3 nearby=1.5 respawn=2.5 round-end=4 click=1 danger=3 pad=1.5 buzz=1.5 zap=2.5
node tools/audio/encode.mjs /tmp/fishtank-audio client/assets/audio
```

`render.rb` is adapted from Sonic Pi's own headless recorder, which pings the daemon every 4 s although the daemon wants a keep-alive more often than every 3 s and otherwise stops the take; this one pings every second and records every piece in a single engine session. Music pieces open with a marker tick and play their loop twice; `encode.mjs` cuts the second pass to the exact loop length (so reverb tails wrap around), trims effects to their sound, peak-normalises to -1 dBFS, and writes AAC (`.m4a`), which every major browser decodes.

## Rendering and mobile resolution

The follow camera sits at `8.6 + r * 1.5` behind the fish, and that second number is the one that decides whether growing is visible at all. It was `r * 3` — three units further back for every one unit of radius gained — which cancelled most of the growth as it happened: across the whole range, mass 8 to the 1800 cap, the player's own fish went from filling 19% of the screen to 46%, so 225 times the mass bought 2.4 times the size, with a hard ceiling of 65% however big you ever got. Halving the retreat keeps the spawn framing where it was and takes a good round from ×1.45 to ×1.60, and the cap to two thirds of the frame. The camera also looks further ahead as the fish lengthens, or a grown one sits in the middle of the view with its own body across it.

Since mass is the cube of the thing you can see, that alone cannot carry the feeling — one small fish is 7% of your radius — so growth is also an event. `createFish` keeps three nodes rather than two: `root` takes the transform the simulation sent, `pose` plays the bite lunge and death tumble, and `swell` exists only for the growth pop. The bite and the pop overlap by design, since you grow the instant you bite, and two animations writing one scaling fight; a node each and they compose. The pop overshoots and settles rather than easing in, scaled by how much was actually gained, so a snack is a nudge and a whole player is a lurch. Every predator pops, not just yours: watching a rival swell is how you learn how big it just got.

The browser downloads JavaScript and renders the aquarium locally on its GPU. Only gameplay state crosses the wire while playing; nothing streams video or meshes to anyone. Preloading affects startup, not the visual fidelity of an already loaded scene.

`client/js/quality.js` owns the graphics setting: one value, one place it is stored, and however many places it is drawn. It presents itself as an `EventTarget` with a `value`, which is deliberate — `world.js`, `sand.js` and `environment.js` were each handed the `<select>` and listened to it for `change`, so keeping that shape meant none of them had to learn that the dropdown became a row of buttons, or that there is now more than one row. Two of them, because the setting is wanted in two places that are never on screen together: the header on the menu, and the pause panel during play, where it is not sitting on the game the rest of the time. Two views of one value, both re-rendered from the module, so they cannot disagree.

It is a dropdown, and a hand-built one, because a `<select>` cannot be this: its popup is drawn by the operating system and is the one part of a form no amount of CSS reaches — which was exactly what gave away that this is a web page. So it is a disclosure button and a list of real buttons in the game's own glass and lime, which also lets each row carry a line explaining itself, as a native `<option>` cannot. Keyboard, focus ring and screen reader still work: arrow keys walk the list and wrap, the current tier takes focus when it opens so a keypress goes somewhere sensible, and Escape closes the list while explicitly _not_ closing the pause panel behind it — dismissing a `<dialog>` is the default action of Escape, so without `preventDefault` one key would take both. `tests/quality.test.js` covers all of that, including that two mounted dropdowns stay in step and that opening one closes the other.

On a landscape phone it stops being a popup. The pause panel's content is taller than the screen there, so the panel has to scroll or Continue and Leave cannot be reached — and a list hanging outside a scrolling box is clipped by it. So under `(max-height: 520px)` the list drops into the panel's own flow and the panel scrolls down to it: the same control, laid out for the room there is. The header's own dropdown stays a popup, and the header sits at `z-index: 4` for it, above the menu and lobby panels at 2 — both were on 2, the header comes first in the document, and on a short screen where the panel reaches up near the header the open list painted behind it.

The tiers are Potato, Battery saver (1 render pixel per CSS pixel), Balanced (up to 1.5), Sharper (up to 2), capped by the display pixel ratio, and Ultra, which supersamples at 1.5× the display's own density (capped at 3×). Each setting also swaps the fish models themselves: every species is authored at four tiers, from a lightweight `-low.glb` silhouette up to an `-hd.glb` carrying roughly three to eight times the geometry as finer scales, rays, and surface detail. **Potato** is the tank as it was in its first week, and it is the tier to reach for on an old tablet or for a laugh: a wireframe frame drawn as twelve lines, a grid on the sand every 6 units, flat untextured colours, the lightest authored fish, and no room at all beyond the glass. It is also the only tier that _adds_ something — those two line systems — rather than taking things away. The savings are mostly not triangles: 279 meshes drop to 81 because the office is 181 little boxes seen only through a pane, the glass panes and the rippling surface go with them (the two things in the tank that cost fill rate rather than geometry), the glow layer switches off, the kelp stops rewriting thousands of vertices twice a frame, and the render target falls to **15% of the pixels Balanced draws**. Everything it turns off is decoration; the hard scenery and the kelp stay exactly where they are, because nothing that stops a fish or hides one may ever be invisible.

The top two settings also dress the room beyond the glass and the sand under the fish: Sharper loads `office-high.glb`, `zapper-high.glb` and `sand-tile-high.glb`, Ultra the denser trio, adding a modeled monitor interface, individual keys, chair upholstery and casters, framed art, window details, a real shell around the filter, and a rippled white coral seabed in place of the flat speckled plane — with curtains, floorboard seams and pendant lighting on top of that at Ultra. The seabed is one seamless 8-unit tile laid nine by nine, every tile a GPU instance of the one loaded mesh, and it is the only asset in the game carrying real images: a 1024-square base colour and grain normal map, embedded in the GLB, which the loader has to move across by hand because the other material converters keep only base colours. Battery saver and Balanced keep the procedural room exactly as it was, only the selected tier is ever in memory, and a failed load leaves the base scene standing — see [the environment detail notes](docs/ENVIRONMENT-DETAILS.md). Models reload on the fly when the setting changes and the choice is remembered. Babylon uses the inverse hardware scaling factor. The initial prototype mistakenly reduced resolution as device pixel ratio increased; this is corrected. Higher resolution can cost frame rate on slower phones. The Blender fish are roughly five times the triangle count of the old procedural spheres but are hardware instanced; lighting is still a simple hemispheric and directional pair.

Automated control tests cover keyboard release, mouse aim without movement, the left thumb moving and stopping the fish through its deadzone ramp, the right thumb aiming without moving it, an aim that holds where it was put, and focus loss dropping both thumbs. A physical Android/iOS check is still needed to assess touch feel and GPU performance.

### Fish quality tiers

All fourteen species have four authored models, switched live through Render quality. The clownfish is the reference the others were built against:

| Setting       | Model                | Visible detail                                                                               |
| ------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Battery saver | `clownfish-low.glb`  | Lightweight body, simple fins and tail wag                                                   |
| Balanced      | `clownfish.glb`      | Rounded tail, curved fins, raised lips and gill covers                                       |
| Sharper       | `clownfish-high.glb` | Scales, fin rays, pearl tail border and fluttering pectoral fins                             |
| Ultra         | `clownfish-hd.glb`   | Fine staggered scales, scalloped tail, denser rays, iris details and stronger wet highlights |

Every species follows that pattern: Low keeps a lightweight silhouette, standard adds curved fins and clear face and gill shapes, Sharper adds species-specific surface detail plus secondary fin motion, and Ultra adds finer detail that holds up in close-ups. These are geometric details and plain colored materials, without external textures. Geometry and materials remain shared between instances; each fish has its own swim animation phase. Every tier has an editable `assets/blender/<kind>*.blend` source and a studio comparison in `docs/<kind>{-low,,-high,-hd}.png`. Higher tiers cost more geometry and pixels, so compare on your target device before choosing a default.

Rebuild any species with `blender --background --factory-startup --python tools/blender/create_fish.py -- --models-only <kinds>`. `create_fish.py` holds the shared body, fin, ray and eye helpers plus the tessellation table; each species' authored layers live in a detail module it dispatches to through `DETAIL_MODULES`:

| Module                | Species                                          | Shared technique                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| `clownfish_detail.py` | clownfish                                        | Scale arcs, scalloped fins, iris filaments              |
| `reef_detail.py`      | blue tang, pufferfish                            | Swept sails, belly ridges, tapered spines               |
| `flowing_detail.py`   | goldfish, betta, angelfish                       | Veil and fantail membranes with layered rays            |
| `patterned_detail.py` | butterflyfish, wrasse, royal gramma, triggerfish | Markings swept in surface space so they sit in the skin |
| `pelagic_detail.py`   | lionfish, seahorse, manta ray, shark             | Non-standard anatomies, curved-spline tails             |

A detail module exposes `enhance(builder, root, kind, detail, palette)` and `tail(builder, parent, kind, detail, base, edge, rays)`, called for every tier above Low; `palette` carries every material built for that species, keyed by name. Species-level history is tracked in [the fish upgrade worklist](docs/FISH-UPGRADE-WORKLIST.md).
